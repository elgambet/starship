Abstract—NASA’s Artemis campaign will bring astronauts
back to the lunar surface in this decade and beyond. Artemis
objectives include exploration, advancing science and
technology, and learning how to work and live on another world
as we prepare for human missions to Mars. NASA is
collaborating with commercial and international partners to
establish the first long-term presence on the Moon with plans to
land thefirst woman and first person of coloron theMoon using
innovative technologies to explore more of the lunar surface
than ever before.
NASA’s Human Landing System (HLS) program, based at
NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama,
is responsible for the development of the vehicles that will take
astronautsfromlunarorbittothesurfaceof theMoonandback,
safely. The program has contracted with two industry
providers, SpaceX and a Blue Origin-led team, to build the next
lunarlanders. In 2021, NASAawarded a firm, fixed-price (FFP)
contracttoSpaceXtoprovideaninitiallunarlanderfor Artemis
III – scheduled to be the first mission to return astronauts to the
Moon in more than 50 years. In 2022, NASA awarded SpaceX a
contract modification to provide a more capable lunar lander
for Artemis IV. In parallel, the program awarded another FFP
contract to Blue Origin and its partners to provide a landing
system for Artemis V, giving NASA a dissimilar, redundant
capability for human Moon landings. For Artemis VI and
beyond, NASA plans to competitively procure landing services.
In late 2023, the HLS program also gave the contracted
companies the authority to begin work to develop large cargo
landers, based on modifications of the human landing systems.
The HLS program is making significant strides with SpaceX
and Blue Origin as the companies perform design, development,
test, and evaluation (DDT&E) of these human landing systems.
The HLS program continues to provide critical oversight,
insight, and expertise into many of the key areas required to
return to the Moon, such as technology and engine development
and cryogenic fluid management. This paper will detail the
progress both providers have made toward Artemis III, IV, and
V as well as large cargo landers, scheduled to become available
no earlier than Artemis VII. We will also discuss the role
Marshall Space Flight Center and the HLS program play in the
evolution of the public-private partnerships within the
aerospace industry and the critical role of NASA and its
engineering expertise in getting humans back to the Moon.

1. INTRODUCTION
NASAregularlysets strategic goalsforitsscienceandhuman
spaceflight programs. One of the agency’s strategic goals is
to extend human presence to the Moon and on to Mars for
sustainable long-term exploration, development, and science
utilization. The Artemis campaign of lunar missions, and the
numerous assets for deep space exploration that the agency’s
Moon to Mars Directorate is developing, will enable NASA
to lead a diverse coalition of nations and organizations back
to the Moon. Having gained significant spaceflight
experience flying cargo and crew to the International Space
Station (ISS) in low-Earth orbit (LEO), commercial
aerospace companies have a key part to play in Artemis. To
leverage this expertise, NASA opted to purchase landing
services from commercial companies for human lunar
landings scheduled for Artemis III and beyond. The Human
Landing System (HLS) program, managed out of NASA’s
Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama,
provides rigorous oversight and insight into engineering
processes, of the two commercial providers selected to
provide landing services for Artemis III-V to ensure the
landing services will meet Artemis requirements.
The Artemis I mission, an uncrewed, rigorous test flight of
the agency’s new super heavy-lift launch vehicle, the Space
Launch System (SLS), and the Orion spacecraft for crew,
launched on November 22, 2022, with Orion splashing down
on December 11, 2022 (see Figure 1). The mission launched
from Launch Complex 39B at NASA’s Kennedy Space

2. PROCURING LANDING SERVICES FOR
ARTEMIS
Using the FFP contracting strategy and procuring landing
services, rather than purchasing government-specified
spacecraft, enables the agency to take advantage of – and
nurture – advances the private sector has made in launch
vehicles and related technology. NASA desired two
commercial companies be awarded contracts for lunar
landing services to give the agency dissimilar, redundant
human landing capabilities.
Safety is a key tenet of NASA’s human spaceflight programs,
including the HLS program. Engineers from the Safety &
Mission Assurance (S&MA) organization work with both
SpaceX and Blue Origin, providing expertise, insight, and
oversight of:
• Human rating requirements
• Hazard analysis development and risk
characterization
• Probabilistic Risk Assessment (PRA) and risk
drivers
• Independent Verification and Validation (IV&V) of
software
In addition to integrating a safety focus into engineering
designs and ground testing and processing procedures, the
HLS program also provides technical expertise to its
commercial providers in the form of agreements known as
Collaboration Tasks. These formalized collaborations allow
NASA to work directly with SpaceX and Blue Origin to
provide expertise in areas that commercial companies may
not have. Another avenue for government-commercial
cooperation and knowledge-sharing is via Government Task
Agreements (GTAs) that enable the commercial companies
to access government resources, such as test facilities, launch
support, simulator/emulator code, and more. HLS manages
several such GTAs with both SpaceX and Blue Origin to help
advance maturity of their landing systems and reduce risk in
key technology development areas needed for Artemis.
3. ARTEMIS III HLS
Following the Artemis II crewed lunar flyby test flight,
Artemis III is scheduled to be the first crewed lunar landing
of the Artemis campaign. NASA has contracted with SpaceX
(Starbase, Tex.) to provide the HLS for Artemis III under the
NextSTEP Appendix H: Human Landing System solicitation
(see Figure 2). NASA set the requirements that the lander
must meet, including:
• Delivery of the HLS to lunar orbit
• A Lunar Orbit Checkout Review (LOCR) in Near-
Rectilinear Halo Orbit (NRHO)
• Docking with Orion in NRHO
• Descent to and landing on the lunar surface (landing
sites are currently being evaluated)
• Support of two crew members on the lunar surface
for six days (one NRHO orbital period)
• Ascent from the lunar surface
• Rendezvous and docking with Orion in NRHO
SpaceX currently launches prototype versions of its Starship
secondstagespacecraftand SuperHeavy launch vehiclefrom
Starbase, Texas. The company also has launch facilities
under construction at NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in
Florida. A significant area of technology development, that
the HLS program and NASA engineers from other
organizations, are assisting with via collaboration and GTAs
is cryogenic refueling in LEO. Cryogenic refueling in LEO is
necessary to enable Starship HLS to land on the Moon with
significant payload capability – and for the company’s
commercial aspirations.
SpaceX has three versions of the Starship upper stage in
development to facilitate cryogenic propellant transfer
operations in LEO for Artemis: depot, tanker, and HLS. Prior
to the Artemis III crewed lunar landing mission, SpaceX will
demonstrate cryogenic propellant transfer operations in Earth
orbit and an uncrewed lunar landing. These two activities are
major milestones in SpaceX’s contract award. The HLS

4. PROGRESS TOWARD ARTEMIS III
Prior to attempting an orbital launch of the Super Heavy-
Starship system, SpaceX conducted a series of Raptor engine
acceptance tests, cryogenic proof and qualification testing on
tank sections, booster and ship engine spin prime testing,
booster and ship hot-fire testing, and wet dress rehearsals.
Test flights of the Starship upper stage then commenced.
Each flight test incorporated lessons learned from the
previous flight, enabling SpaceX to rapidly and iteratively
mature systems, designs, technology, and processes. In May
2021, the Starship upper stage achieved a flight apogee of
33,000 ft. (10 km).
SpaceX began its orbital launch campaign with the fully
stacked, integrated Super Heavy-Starship launch vehicle on
April 20, 2023 (see Figure 3). The vehicle lifted off,
performed ascent operations, and achieved an apogee of
about 24 miles (39 km) before the Flight Termination System
(FTS) activated, destroying the vehicle. A second orbital
flight test of the integrated system launched on November18,
2023. This test marked the first time all 33 Raptor engines
successfully ignited and completed full-duration burns. In
addition, for this flight SpaceX began to employ hot-stage
separation to separate the booster and upper stage. Starship
reached an altitude of about 93 miles (150 km) and orbital
velocity during this test.
SpaceX has also been refining designs on the interior of the
Starship to support HLS requirements. Early progress on the
crew compartment has included construction of a mock-up
crew cabin. This full-scale mock-up is being used for human
factors evaluation of aspects of the Environment Control Life
Support Systems (ECLSS) and thermal control system. In
addition, astronaut crews have provided feedback on training
activities that assess landing trajectories and aspects of
vehicle piloting during landing. Another area of focus has
been material flammability testing, with results feeding a
trade study evaluating various atmospheres for the cabin.
SpaceX has also performed development testing and analyses
on crew displays, the elevator that will take crew from the
airlock deck to the lunar surface and back again, solar array
deployment, thermal and micro-meteoroid orbital debris
(MMOD)protectiontiles,landinglegs,dockingmechanisms,
landing software and sensors, medical systems, and more.

NASA Tipping Point award. The on-orbit demonstration

SpaceX designs for the Starship HLS include an elevator to
take crew from the airlock deck, located near the top of the

elevator controls, gate latches, ramp deployment interfaces

6. HLS PROGRESS TOWARD ARTEMIS IV
Human landing missions after Artemis III will require a more
capable lander than the initial lander that SpaceX will
provide. In 2022, the agency awarded SpaceX a contract
modification to provide an HLS that meets a more robust set
of requirements for Artemis IV. Artemis IV and subsequent
missions will require the HLS to land a crew of up to four and
have the ability to operate for extended durations at the lunar
south pole as well to land at non-polar sites. The HLS for
sustaining missions will also require additional performance
to enable increased up-mass and down-mass, and extended
EVA duration and frequency. All missions after Artemis III
will require the HLS to dock with Gateway.
To support docking with Gateway, SpaceX performed
qualification testing on the Starship docking system, which is
based on the flight-proven Dragon 2 docking system. The
Starship docking system can be configured to dock with
Orion as well as Gateway. The testing, performed at Johnson
Space Center, included more than 200 docking scenarios,
with various approach angles and speeds evaluated. Full-
scale dynamic testing demonstrated “soft capture”
capabilities while in the active docking role with the soft
capture system (SCS) of the active docking system extended
toward the passive system.
7. PROGRESS TOWARD ARTEMIS V
In May 2023, NASA awarded Blue Origin (Kent, Wash.) and
its partners – Lockheed Martin (Bethesda, Md)., Draper
(Cambridge, Mass.), Astrobotic (Pittsburgh, Penn.), and
Honeybee Robotics (Brooklyn, N. Y.) – a contract to develop
an HLS to meet sustaining mission requirements and to be
used for Artemis V. Blue Origin and its partners are
developing the Blue Moon MK2 lander for Artemis V (see
Figure 7). The HLSprogram and Blue Originhave completed
several early milestones, primarily focused on requirements,
design and construction standards, interface definitions,
integration, and design reviews. An early effort known as
“standards adjudication” was completed in December 2023
for the Blue Origin team. NASA’s HLS program and Blue
Origin agreed to a set of standards that included some NASA
standards and some provider standards that meet the intent of
the NASA standards. In November 2023, the HLS program
and Blue Origin completed a Certification Baseline Review
(CBR). For CBR, NASA reviewed contract deliverables that
provided early verification of compliance to requirements.
Some of the CBR deliverables provided by the Blue Origin
team and reviewed by the HLS program and independent
reviewers included:
• Specifications
• Verification objectives
• Concept of operations (ConOps)
Following successful completion of the standards
adjudication process and CBR, the HLS program and Blue
Origin held a Preliminary Design Review (PDR) in February
2024that covered design and analysis of the integrated lander
architecture. In addition, Blue Origin delivered a low-fidelity
mock-up of its HLS crew module in 2023 at its facility in
Kent,Wash.Thereplicaincludessimulated crewsystemsand
flight deck. The lunar crew module mock-up will serve as a
testbed for subsystem development, HITL evaluations,
integrated analysis, and configuration testing. Blue Origin
also built a low-fidelity lander training simulator in 2023.
This virtual reality-based simulator, located at NASA’s
Johnson Space Center, supports assessments of crew module
design and lunar landing operations feasibility.

8. LARGE HUMAN-CLASS CARGO
DELIVERY SERVICES
In late 2023, the HLS Program exercised contract
modifications to enable SpaceX and Blue Origin to begin
work on large-scale cargo landers to deliver 12-15 t payloads
to the lunar surface (see Figures 10, 11). Cargo delivery
services ensure NASA can deploy multidisciplinary science
and technology tools, instruments, and payloads to the lunar
surface. Design of these human-class cargo delivery landers
leverages designs of the companies’ HLS landers with an
expected 80% common functionality overlap, but includes
modified payload interfaces, elements for offloading cargo,
and deployment mechanisms.
ThecurrentcontractsincludedeliveryfromEarthand are
not dependent on the timing of any Artemis missions. The
large cargo landers are required to be able to operate
autonomously as well as with crew control. Because delivery
of these large payloads is not tied to any specific Artemis
mission, they could be landed on the Moon between crewed
Artemis missions.
9. CONCLUSION
Following three decades focused on learning to live and work
in LEO aboard the ISS, NASA is focused on putting boots on
the Moon with Artemis -- with eyes toward Mars. The HLS
program, is applying lessons learned from the Commercial
Crew program, working with commercial providers to
provide lunar crew transportation services using FFP
contracts. Once astronauts have safely landed on the Moon
with Artemis, science activities during surface EVAs will
usher in a new era of discovery, for the benefit of all.
